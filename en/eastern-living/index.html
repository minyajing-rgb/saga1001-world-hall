Eastern Living Wisdom / English Journal

An easier way to end the day.

Seven Chinese-inspired home rituals: a warm cup, a quieter table, a comfortable bowl and space to exhale.

Ancient Chinese wisdom you can actually use tonight.

Cultural editorial article. No digital book is currently offered for purchase on this page.

Why do so many Chinese families keep a thermos nearby, serve rice congee, or talk about a simple evening foot bath? These habits are part of everyday family life. They do not belong to one universal Chinese routine, and they are not medical cures. Their appeal can be much simpler: warmth, time and a sense of coming home.

This guide offers seven optional ways to borrow the feeling of those rituals. Many of the underlying habits—tea, daylight, air circulation, regular rest—are familiar around the world. The distinctively Chinese part is the cultural context and the way ordinary objects hold meaning.

Seven small rituals to try

  1. 01 / CLEAR
    Put away one unfinished task and clear a single surface.
  2. 02 / SIP
    Enjoy a small cup of comfortably warm drinking water. Room-temperature water is also fine.
  3. 03 / BREW
    Try a simple cup of mint tea with food-grade leaves.
  4. 04 / REST
    If safe for you, consider a brief foot soak in comfortably warm, not hot, plain water.
  5. 05 / COOK
    Prepare a warm bowl of rice congee with everyday ingredients.
  6. 06 / NOTICE
    Enjoy the first few bites of a meal without a screen.
  7. 07 / WRITE
    Close the evening with a single sentence about what you want to set down.

Warm water: a cultural habit, not a health promise

A Chinese-style thermos is an object almost anyone can understand. For some families it is associated with care, hospitality and the practical comfort of a warm drink. You do not need a special tea blend. Use water at a temperature you personally enjoy and avoid scalding drinks.

How to make a simple mint infusion

Wash three food-grade mint leaves, add them to a clean mug, pour in hot drinking water, and allow the drink to cool to a comfortable temperature. Treat it as a culinary infusion rather than a treatment for digestive, respiratory or sleep problems. If you have allergies, take medication or are pregnant, check ingredients before experimenting with concentrated herbal products.

Foot-soaking: the comfort and the caution

Evening foot baths appear in many Chinese home-wellness discussions. If you have healthy skin and normal foot sensation, a short soak in comfortably warm plain water can be a pleasant personal ritual. Dry your feet thoroughly afterwards. Avoid very hot water and stop if you feel uncomfortable.

Safety first: The US CDC advises people with diabetes not to soak their feet. If you have reduced sensation, poor circulation, sores or infection, seek professional advice instead of self-directed soaking. Do not present foot baths or added herbs as treatments.

A small bowl of rice congee

Rinse half a cup of rice and simmer it with approximately four cups of water for 40–60 minutes, stirring and adding water when needed until the grains soften. Finish with ordinary vegetables or scallions if desired. Congee is food and a cultural comfort; it does not replace a balanced diet or medical care.

“Eat in quiet, rest in quiet”

The Analects, Book 10 (Xiang Dang), records the phrase 食不语，寝不言 (shi bu yu, qin bu yan). In its historical context this is a description of conduct. Modern readers can take it as a prompt to be present with a meal—not as a clinical rule, or a reason to ban warm family conversation. Read the Chinese primary text.

The 15-minute version

Use three minutes to clear a small space; three minutes to prepare water or tea; five minutes to sit quietly; then write one line in a notebook. If a foot soak is not suitable, skip it entirely. The sequence is a suggestion, not a daily performance target.

Try tonight: “Today, I want to set down…” Write the rest of the sentence in your own words. Nothing else needs to be completed.
